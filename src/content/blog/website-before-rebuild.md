A website redesign is often the first thing a business reaches for when enquiries feel slow. It is visible, it feels like progress and it is easy to put in a brief. But a new website only changes the front door. If the follow-up process behind that door is broken, the same problems tend to reappear a few weeks after launch.

## Start with what happens after the form

Before looking at design, map the journey of a single enquiry. Where does it land? Who sees it? How quickly? What happens if that person is busy or on leave? What happens if the enquiry is incomplete?

Many teams discover that the website was never the bottleneck. The real issue is that enquiries sit in an inbox, get copied into a spreadsheet, or depend on one person remembering to reply. A cleaner website can even make this worse by increasing the number of enquiries that fall through the same gap.

## Signs the process needs work

A few practical signals are worth checking:

- Enquiries arrive in more than one place and nobody owns the full list.
- Response time depends on who happens to check the inbox.
- There is no agreed next step after the first reply.
- Follow-up is manual and only happens when someone has time.
- Nobody can say how many enquiries became conversations last month.

If several of these are true, a new website will not solve them on its own. It may improve first impressions, but the operational problem remains.

## Fix the handoff first

The good news is that the fix is often smaller than a full rebuild. It might be a shared inbox with clear ownership, a simple CRM pipeline, an automatic acknowledgement, or a notification that reaches the right person immediately.

The goal is to make the process visible. Once every enquiry has a stage and an owner, it becomes obvious where things stall. That information is more valuable than a new homepage.

## Then decide what the website needs to do

With the process fixed, the website brief becomes clearer. Instead of "make it look better", the question becomes "what does this page need to help someone do?" That usually leads to a smaller, more focused build: clearer service pages, a shorter form, better proof and a more obvious next step.

A website is a useful part of the system. It is not the whole system. When the two are designed together, the improvement is visible in how quickly and consistently enquiries are handled — not just in how the site looks on launch day.
